What is Blue? The impact of homophily on consensus formation

Seoul Lee & Uwaila Ekhator

Introduction

Things in the world are more or less similar to one another. Based on their relative proximities on a continuous feature space, people develop mental schemas to categorize things. Since there is no necessary relationship between categorization schemas and the feature space, people from different planets or cultures may have different categorization schemas. A classic example of this cultural variation is the naming of colors. While colors exist on a continuum, people use discrete categories to identify and denote different groups of color shades. For example, in the old Korean language, the word for blue was also used to denote green, while modern Koreans differentiate between blue and green with distinct terms. This example suggests that individuals with different categorization schemas may face difficulties in communication, and that these schemas are subject to change over time.

In this project, we explore a scenario where individuals from different planets bring different categorization schemas. Specifically, we examine the problem of what to call "blue." While people from different planets may use different terms to denote the concept of blue, we assume that all already have some concept of blue, though the range of shades it covers may vary.

In our model, agents update their own categorization schema by observing how others categorize a given stimulus. We show how they reach consensus on what to call “blue” over time. In addition, we introduce a behavioral assumption that agents are more influenced by the categorizations of others who share similar physical traits. We compare the outcome of the model with this behavioral assumption with that of the baseline model, where each agent puts equal weights on others’ categorization.

Baseline model

For each time step, a random color, which is represented as a 3-dimensional vector (e.g., RGB), is given to all agents. Each agent judges whether the given color is blue (1) or not (0), using a logistic model. In the initial step, each agent’s model parameters (i.e., β0, β1, β2, β3) are randomly initiated.

Then, each agent updates their own model parameters based on others’ judgments. Specifically, they treat the average of others’ judgments as the label for the given stimulus and use gradient descent for parameter updates. This process is repeated over multiple steps (100 steps). For each step, conformity is measured as the proportion agreeing with the majority.

We assume a fully connected network of 10 agents, which means that every agent can observe every other agent. This allows them to calculate the average of others’ judgments. For the baseline model, we assume all agents apply an equal learning rate (0.1) for gradient descent. All results below present the average of 1,000 simulations, with shaded areas indicating  standard deviation.

Baseline model Results

After running the baseline model for 100 steps with a learning rate of 0.1, we observed that the agents arrived at a consensus at around step 40 (figure 1), but when the learning rate was reduced to 0.05, the agents arrived at a consensus much later around step 80 (figure 2). This shows that learning rate (openness to change) impacts the speed at which agents are able to come to an agreement on a categorization schema.

Homophily Assumption

Homophily is the tendency for people to be attracted to those who are similar to them. In our homophily model, we assume that people from different planets may have different or similar physical traits, since they are chosen at random. e.g., number of legs, number of eyes, perceived age. So we assume that people are more likely to adopt a schema (colour choice) from someone with the same physical trait.

Homophily model Specifications

Each agent updates their own model parameters based on homophily. We assumed an equal number of agents in each group: 5 agents each have the same trait.

We created two kinds of homophily model:

  • Model 1 (Weights) : In this model, each agent updates their own model parameters based on others’ judgements. However, here a weighted average is utilized, agents assign higher weights to those who share the same physical traits.
  • Model 2 (Learning rates):  In this model, agents calculate the average judgment separately for each group (i.e., two averages and two updates for each step). They calculate an average for the group with similar physical traits with them and another one for the group different from them. Then, they apply a higher learning rate when updating based on the average judgment from agents with the same physical traits.

Homophily model 1 (weights) Results

After running the homophily-weighted model, we observed that the agents failed to reach consensus, unlike in the baseline model where they eventually converged on a single opinion (figure 3). We also noticed that when we tried different multiplying factors (x1 to x3) for the agents with similar traits, there was an obvious difference from x2 above, with reductions in average conformity levels (figure 4).

Figure 3: Homophily weighted model result with x2 weights assigned to similar agents

Figure 4:  Homophily weighted model showing results for various multiplying factors

Homophily model 2 (learning rate) Results

After running the homophily learning rate model, we observed that the agents failed to reach consensus which is similar to what we observed in the weighted model (figure 5). We also tried a range of learning rates for dissimilar agents (from 90% to 50% of the learning rate for similar agents), and we observed that for 60% and above, agents would eventually arrive at a consensus (figure 6). However, when the learning rate for dissimilar agents became 50%, they failed to reach consensus.

We also doubled the learning rates, and ran the model again (figure 7), this gave similar results as figure 6. Notably, when the learning rate for dissimilar agents was 50%, i.e., 0.1 for dissimilar agents and 0.2 for similar agents, they did not arrive at consensus, even though both learning rates were greater than or equal to the uniform learning rate of 0.1 in the baseline model, which successfully achieved a consensus. This result shows that the key factor in whether a group of agents can reach a consensus is not the absolute level of the learning rate, but the relative difference in learning rates between factional subgroups.

Questions/Future Direction

Some further questions that can be explored with our model include:

  1. How does behaviour change if agents must choose among multiple colours instead of binary (blue/not-blue)?
  2. What happens if we consider out-group aversion, does this lead to polarization?
  3. What happens when the number of agents in each homophily group is unbalanced?
  4. What happens when we include more homophily e.g. other physical traits all together?

Applications to Social Science

Some ways this model can be applied to social science include:

  1. This model can be used for any belief-adoption process by setting the binary output to ‘accepts vs. rejects’ a claim e.g. climate change, vaccines, etc.
  2. This model can be used to predict the conformity level within a group with multiple factional subgroups (e.g., gender, race, educational background, political ideology), if there is a tendency for homophily.
  3. This model can be extended to explore how different communication structures influence group conformity. While the current model assumes a fully connected network, where each agent observes the judgments of all others, future research could investigate the effects of alternative network topologies. For example, to reflect hierarchical structures within organizations, a scale-free network could be considered, where hub agents have access to more judgments than those on the periphery.

Conclusion

We considered the situation where people from different planets bring their own categorization schemas and attempt to reach a consensus on what to call “blue.” We modeled this situation by assuming that each agent uses a logistic model to classify a randomly given color into blue or not. We also assumed that they update their model parameters (i.e., categorization schema) by observing others’ judgments. We compared the baseline model where each agent considers all others’ categorization equally and the models with the homophily assumption that agents are more influenced by those who share similar physical traits.

Our results show that when they equally consider others’ categorization, they eventually arrive at a consensus, while the convergence speed was dependent on the learning rate, which represents their openness to change. In contrast, in the homophily models, we found that a perfect consensus could not be reached when there is a significant tendency for homophily. Specifically, consensus failed to emerge when agents assigned half or less weight to those with different traits (Homophily Model 1), or when they used a learning rate that was half or less when updating from dissimilar individuals (Homophily Model 2). These findings suggest that when individuals systematically discount others’ judgments based on perceived dissimilarity, it becomes significantly harder for the group to achieve a shared categorization schema.
