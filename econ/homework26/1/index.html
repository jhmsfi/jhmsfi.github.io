Cooperative Search under Heterogeneous, Unknown Preferences
by Vrinda Malhotra and Stefan Weiland
28.05,2026, CSS Workshop, Santa Fe Institute
There are many real-world scenarios in which various agents or species cohabitate in an
ecosystem, their payoH (or utility) environments diHer and are ex ante (partially)
unknown, and their decisions are interdependent. For example: (1) humans and aliens
coexisting on a planet, each with opaque preferences and technologies, as well as an
interdependence on the respective other; (2) humans of diHerent cultures sharing a city,
institution, or resource, where each cultural group has its own norms and preferences yet
their joint decisions over shared spaces determine collective outcomes; (3)
intercropping communities, in which neighboring farmers choose which crops to plant,
when to irrigate, and how to manage pests, without full knowledge of their own and each
other's agronomic conditions.
If payoH levels were independent across groups, the optimization problem would play out
at the individual level: through trial-and-error search, each agent identiﬁes a
conﬁguration of choices that maximizes, or at least improves, her payoH. Conversely, if
payoH levels are interdependent across agents, each agent must consider other agents'
choices to maximize their own payoH, making unilateral optimization insuHicient. In the
latter case, cooperation is beneﬁcial.
Both in the presence and in the absence of interdependencies across agents, optimal
search requires a willingness to suHer temporary losses - going downhill - to escape local
peaks and identify superior conﬁgurations. In the current project, we examine whether
the number of such downhill moves is higher in interdependent settings than in purely
individual ones, and by how much. Further, we examine how the answer to this question
varies with the complexity of the search environment, i.e., diHerent degrees of interaction
in the payoH landscape.
We assume two agents, A and B, representing diHerent groups or species. The individual
payoH environments of agents A and B are each generated using the NK rugged
landscape technique. Both landscapes consist of 10 input dimensions with 2 choices per
dimension. The general characteristics of the payoH environments are identical across
both agents - they share the same number K of dependencies across dimensions - but
the speciﬁc realizations diHer. PayoH interdependence among agents is introduced via a
third NK landscape that takes as inputs the individual payoffs of A and B and determines
a joint performance modifier, thereby coupling their respective optimization problems.
Each agent's objective is to maximize individual payoH. The payoH environments are ex
ante unknown to both agents. The timing is dynamic and discrete. The search strategy is
random, local search.
The numerical procedure is as follows:
1. CONVERGE — both agents hill-climb independently until neither can improve
their payoH through any single-dimension change. We call the number of steps
they can wait for “patience”.
1. COORDINATE — both agents exchange one binary signal: "I am stuck."
2. SACRIFICE — each agent independently takes one random downhill bit-ﬂip
simultaneously, with no ﬁtness values shared and no direction inferred.
3. Repeat — return to Step 1. The procedure terminates when the joint optimum W*
is reached, 60 sacriﬁce rounds are exhausted, or 20,000 total steps are exceeded.
We compute the number of sacriﬁce rounds required to reach W* and compare it to the
benchmark scenario in which a single agent searches over an individual landscape in
isolation. The comparison is repeated across diHerent values of K. For each parameter
setting, results are averaged over 10000 independent simulation runs to ensure
robustness.

The outcome is reported in Figure 1. We ﬁnd that the count of sacriﬁce rounds - and thus
the aggregate cost of search - is strictly higher under interdependence than under
individual search, and increasing in K. The intuition is straightforward: when agent A's
payoH depends on agent B's choices, a locally improving move for A may inadvertently
alter the landscape experienced by B, displacing her from a previously identiﬁed peak.
This externality forces additional downhill moves that would not arise under individual
search. The eHect grows with K, as higher ruggedness produces more numerous and
sharper local peaks, increasing the probability that a neighbor's move renders a
previously stable position suboptimal. What is surprising is that the number of necessary
temporaneous sacriﬁces under interdependent search exceeds the number under
individual search by a factor of 2.5 to 4. This, in turn, suggests that cooperative search
requires a disproportionately greater willingness to accept temporary losses than
individual search. Further, it provides an explanation for why, in empirical settings,
diHerent groups cohabitating in a shared environment are likely to settle into local traps.
Another result of our analysis is that coordination requires patience. Because our agents
explore diHerent payoH environments, they converge to local optima at diHerent times
and thus must wait for the other agent to converge. That delay is not free: each round in
which the agent waits for the other to converge implies potentially giving up higher payoff
levels, which is structurally equivalent to sacrifices through a downhill climb.

The ﬁndings carry several practical and theoretical implications. First, they suggest that
the cost of coordination is structurally embedded in the complexity of the shared
environment. Policymakers and institutional designers should therefore expect
coordination to be inherently more costly in complex, highly interdependent settings, and
should allocate resources accordingly. Second, the result that even minimal
communication - a single binary signal - is suHicient to sustain productive cooperation
highlights the value of simple coordination protocols in settings where rich information
exchange is infeasible, as is typical across cultural or species boundaries. Third, the
monotone relationship between K and the cost of coordination implies that ecosystem
or organizational interventions that reduce the degree of payoH interdependence -
through modularity, partitioning of shared resources, or decoupling of decisions - can
meaningfully, and disproportionately more than in individual search problems, reduce
the burden of collective search. Fourth, collaboration requires patience, but that
patience can yield higher payoHs in the long run.
We hope that our research informs human and alien species alike in organizing peaceful,
productive cohabitation if and when necessary, in which heterogeneous preferences are
mutually respected and even leveraged. Further, we hope that our research informs other
settings in which diHerent groups or species manage a shared ecosystem under ex ante
unknown preferences or payoHs.
